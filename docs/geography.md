# Geography of the Ocnele Mari Region (Vâlcea County, Romania)

**Owner:** 01 · Lead and story (Mario)

*Research compiled on 14 November 2026 from open web sources. Statements are paraphrased from the sources listed at the end. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is shown rather than resolved (see "Discrepancies").*

---

## 1. Summary

Ocnele Mari is a small salt-mining town in the **Vâlcea Subcarpathians** (Getic Subcarpathians), in Oltenia, southern Romania. It sits in the middle and upper part of the **Sărata stream** catchment, a tributary basin of the **Olt River**, about 10 km from the county capital Râmnicu Vâlcea. Its geography is defined by three overlapping features:

1. A **fold structure** (the Ocnele Mari–Govora anticline) of weakly resistant Miocene sandy-clayey rocks.
2. A **large salt body** inside that anticline, exploited since prehistory and, since the 1950s–60s, by water injection through wells.
3. **Strong slope and ground instability**, both natural (landslides, gullying) and human-induced (collapse craters, sinkhole lakes, subsidence).

## 3. Physical geography

### 3.1 Regional setting

- The Vâlcea Subcarpathians lie on both sides of the Olt River and form a subdivision of the Getic Subcarpathians, roughly one third of their area.
- Vâlcea County steps down from mountains in the north, through Subcarpathian hills and foothills, to plain-like areas in the south. The Olt valley is the main north–south passage and the county's main river.

### 3.2 Geological structure

- The landscape is controlled by the **Ocnele Mari–Govora anticline**, part of the Dacian Basin, built mostly of **Miocene sandy-clayey deposits**.
- The **salt deposit** is of **Badenian** age, stratiform-lenticular, runs roughly east–west on the northern flank of the anticline, and dips northwards (about 5–35°). Its roof is irregular, partly because of diapirism, and is capped by salt breccia.
- Published dimensions differ: about 2,500 m × 1,650 m with thickness 24–327 m (Mesescu, 2011), versus thickness up to about 450 m at the centre (Ene et al., 2009).
- The upper part of the deposit contains several **sterile (non-salt) intercalations**, 0.5–20 m thick. These redirected the dissolution in wells and allowed uncontrolled linking of cavities, which is central to the later collapses.
- The salt is covered by tuffitic marls and slightly permeable clays; alluvium in the valley tops (especially along the Sărata) lets surface water percolate toward the upper salt and dissolve it.

### 3.3 Relief: three sectors of the Sărata valley

According to Ene, Osaci-Costache and Tîrlă (2009), the study area divides into:

1. **Headwater sector** upstream of Lunca: narrow, with slope breaks marking the contact between Tortonian marls and Sarmatian deposits.
2. **Erosional depression** between Lunca and Ocnele Mari, about 5 km long, centred on the Ocnița syncline and the southern flank of the Urziceni syncline, bordered by the anticline. Here the floodplain, terraces and proluvial glacis are best developed. Most of the settlement and the collapse fields are in or beside this sector.
3. **Transverse sector** between Ocnele Mari and Copăcelu (about 1.7 km): the stream cuts through the hard rocks of the anticline axis (tuffs, sandstones, microconglomerates), giving a narrow valley with many structural and rock-resistance scarps.

### 3.4 Slope processes

- Lower slopes sit on the **Lower Tortonian–Sarmatian marly complex**: moderate gradients, many **landslides**.
- Upper slopes sit on the **Middle–Upper Sarmatian sandy complex**: steeper, with **gullying** as the most active process.
- Landslide-prone slopes are reactivated periodically, and the collapse lakes have added bank failures of their own.

### 3.5 Elevation

I found no reliable published town elevation. One useful reference point: the lowest well/cavern in Field II (well 379) lay at about 285 m a.s.l. Terrain around the settlement is therefore in the low-hill range of the Subcarpathians.

## 4. Hydrography

- The local axis is the **Sărata (Sărat) stream**, which drains toward the **Olt River**. A map in Mesescu (2011) also shows the Bistrița, Olănești and Topolog creeks joining the Olt near the industrial intakes at Râmnicu Vâlcea.
- Since the 1970s the Olt has been regulated into a **chain of reservoirs** (including Govora, Băbeni, Râureni and Râmnicu Vâlcea). Industrial water intakes for the chemical plants at Râmnicu Vâlcea lie downstream of the salt area.
- **Lakes in the area** (Ene et al., 2009):
  - *Old quarry lakes* (abandoned in the late 19th century): **Lacul Sărat** and **Lacul Dulce**, nearly circular, fed by rain, snow and groundwater.
  - *Ocnița collapse lakes* (after the old mine closed in 1963 and flooded): Ocnița C and I (1968), II (end of 1969), III (1972). Of the original four depressions, three remain visible.
  - *Țeica complex*, formed from 2001 on the slope of Dealul Țeica, left of the Sărata.
- Salt-rich water is a long-term hazard to local surface water and groundwater.

## 5. Climate

**No station series for Ocnele Mari itself was found.** The figures below come from nearby or regional sources and should be treated as indicative only.

- Regional character: temperate-continental, typical of the Subcarpathian belt.
- Băbeni (nearby Vâlcea town, modelled data on Climate-Data.org): mean annual temperature about **10.5 °C**, about **900 mm** annual precipitation, Köppen Cfb.
- Sălătrucel (farther north in the county, peer-reviewed land-use study): about 9.7 °C annual mean, −1.5 °C in January, 19 °C in July.
- Commercial weather sites list May–June as the wettest months and January–February as the coldest for Ocnele Mari, with summer highs near 28 °C. These are consumer-grade averages.
- Heavy rain is a recurring trigger: the 2001 collapse followed a period of heavy rain, and later brine releases followed episodes of intense precipitation.

Localities (2011 → 2021, partial list): Gura Suhașului 1,513 → 1,595; Ocnița 526 → 477; Lunca 542 → 452; Slătioarele 509 → 452; Cosota 120 → 75; Buda 88 → 77.

### 6.2 Settlement pattern

A compact town core plus small villages strung along the Sărata valley and the slopes of the surrounding hills. Several villages (Lunca, Ocnița, Gura Suhașului, Țeica area) sit close to former or active salt-extraction fields, which is why mining hazards translate directly into settlement risk.

### 6.3 Economy and land use

- **Salt**: extraction since the Neolithic; systematic modern exploitation at Ocnița's "Salina Veche" from the mid-19th century until 1895; Mina "Pavel" opened in 1937 (later "1 Mai"), operating until 1963; a newer mine at Coceneşti opened in 1993.
- **Brine for industry**: water-injection wells supplied the chemical industry (Govora and Oltchim, Râmnicu Vâlcea) from the 1950s–60s.
- **Tourism and balneology**: the former mine is now a visitor attraction with an underground church, sports areas, a museum and a restaurant; a salt-water bathing area ("bottomless" pool) occupies a former mine mouth; a Dacian fortress (Buridava) lies in the wider area. Press reports cite 35,000 visitors in a single month in 2013.
- Land reclaimed from closed sonde fields is promoted by the municipality for investors.

## 7. The mining hazard: collapse geography

### 7.1 Extraction fields

Five extraction fields were set up (I–V in some sources; four in others). Field I operated 1961–1974; Field II began in 1968 (15 wells in a triangular grid, brine extraction from 1971) and was shut down in 1993; Fields III and IV continued.

### 7.2 Why Field II failed

Cylindrical chambers planned at about 100 m diameter with 50 m pillars and a 70 m roof. Because of the sterile intercalations, dissolution did not follow the design: chambers merged into groups, then into a single large cavern. A 1993 survey measured a cavern about 60 m high, about 100,000 m² in cross-section and about 4.4 million m³ in volume.

### 7.3 Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1963–1972 | Old Ocnița mine flooded (1963); collapse lakes Ocnița C, I, II, III form (1968–1972) |
| 1993 | Field II extraction stops; German firm SOCON surveys the cavern |
| 12 Sep 2001 | Roof over wells 365 and 367 collapses; about 1.7 million m³ of brine flows down Dealul Țeica; 8 m deep ravine; initial depression about 100 m, later enlarged to about 300 m; about 10 households destroyed; brine reaches the Olt; downstream salinity reportedly over 30× admissible limits; chemical plants cut output by about 30% |
| Days after | More than 100 households resettled; retention dam (about 150,000 m³) built |
| 2002 | Insulation fluid (diesel) pumped out; September 2002 new overflow after well 356 collapses, linking Field I and II; about 60 households and 200 people relocated |
| 2003 | Technical closure plan approved (controlled roof collapse, stream regulation, dam repair, demolition and resettlement, ecological restoration) |
| 13 Jul 2004 | Salt blocks fall from the roof; about 1 million m³ of brine overflows, mostly held in the retention basin |
| Aug–Dec 2005 | Controlled collapse; on 14–24 Dec a sinking cone grows from 20 m to over 130 m, about 105 ha affected |
| 2004–2006 | Monitoring in Field I: roof under 10 m thick over about 3,250 m², sinking about 20 cm/year; collapse forecast by about 2010 |

| 2010–2012 | Satellite (InSAR) and topographic monitoring; experiments backfilling the cavern of well 361 |
| 2013 | Local officials describe only slight residual movements and about 150 ha of land to be returned for development |

### 7.4 Scale of impact (different counts)

- Mesescu (2011): collapses over about 10 ha; 113 households and 209 persons affected.
- Ene et al. (2009): in 2001–2002, about 90 houses flooded twice, over 200 people evacuated; about 1,500 people in Lunca, Ocnița, Gura Suhașului and Cosota exposed to future risk; clean-up estimated at over €15 million; extraction foreseen to continue at least 30 more years in the active fields.
- Mesescu also notes 21 historic-monument sites, including churches, endangered by land instability.

### 7.5 Monitoring

- Microseismic sensors installed after 2001 recorded ongoing small caving events (magnitude about 1–1.4).
- Topographic reference points were installed in Field I (2014–2015).
- InSAR work exists from TerraSAR-X (post-2007), including a 2010 deformation map. **No InSAR data for 2001 or 2005 were found** (earlier ERS/Envisat-era studies were not located).
